A practice-area page is often the first page a visitor lands on from a search. They arrive with a specific problem and a few specific questions. The page works when it answers those questions in plain language and then shows a clear next step.

## Start with the questions, not the page

Before writing, list the questions you hear on the first phone call for this kind of matter. Your intake notes and your own memory are the right source. For most areas, the list looks something like this:

- Is this the kind of thing you help with?
- What usually happens first?
- How long does the process tend to take?
- What should I gather before we talk?
- How do I get in touch, and what happens when I do?

Those questions become the outline of the page.

## A simple structure

1. **A short opening.** One or two sentences that name the situation in the visitor's words and say the firm helps with it.
2. **Who this page is for.** A few examples of the situations you see. Keep them general. Do not describe a real client's matter.
3. **How the process usually works.** The common steps, in order. Say that every matter is different. Avoid exact timelines unless they are set by a rule and you are sure they apply.
4. **What to have ready.** Documents, dates, and names that make a first conversation more useful.
5. **Common questions.** Short answers to the questions from your list.
6. **How to reach the firm.** The phone number, the contact form, and what happens after someone reaches out.

## Keep it general information

A practice-area page explains how things tend to work. It should not read as advice for the reader's own situation. Phrases such as "in many cases" and "this depends on the facts" are honest, not weak. Ending a section with "the details of your situation matter, so it helps to talk it through" points the reader to a conversation instead of a conclusion.

## Words to watch

Plain language helps everyone. If you need a legal term, explain it the first time it appears. "Probate, the court process for settling an estate" is clearer than "probate" alone.

Also watch the claims. Most state bars have rules about how a lawyer describes their own work. Describing what the firm does is fine. Promising an outcome, comparing yourself to other firms, or implying a credential you do not hold is where trouble starts. Check your state's rules when in doubt.

## One page per real area

If the firm handles three distinct areas, three focused pages usually serve visitors better than one long page that covers all of them. Each page can answer its own questions without making the reader scroll past matters that do not apply to them.

## A last check

Read the page as someone who has never hired a lawyer. Did it answer the question that brought you there? Do you know what to do next? If yes, the page is ready.
