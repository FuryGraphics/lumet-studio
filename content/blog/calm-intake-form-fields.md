The contact form on a firm site is often where a visitor decides whether to reach out at all. People who fill it in are usually dealing with something stressful. A short, calm form makes that first step easier.

## Fields that help

These are enough for most firms to respond well:

- **Name.** One field is fine. Splitting first and last name adds a step without much benefit.
- **Phone number.**
- **Email address.**
- **Preferred way to be contacted.** Phone, email, or text, if the firm can respond by text.
- **A short description of what's going on.** One open text box with a gentle prompt such as "Tell us briefly what you need help with."

If the firm handles several areas, a short dropdown for the type of matter can help route the message. Keep the list short and include "Not sure" or "Something else."

## Fields that get in the way

These are common on law firm forms and usually better saved for a later step:

- Full mailing address
- Date of birth
- Detailed questions about the other party
- Long checklists of facts
- Required fields that ask how the person found the firm

Each one is a reason to stop filling in the form. The firm can ask for these details after the first conversation, when the person knows who they are talking to.

## Mark only what's truly required

Make name and at least one way to reach the person required. Leave the rest optional. If a field is optional, say so, and do not block the form when it is empty.

## Set expectations next to the form

A sentence or two near the form does a lot of work:

- Say who will read the message and roughly when the firm usually responds, as long as you can keep that promise.
- Ask the person not to send confidential details until the firm has followed up. Many firms also note that sending the form does not by itself make the firm their lawyer. Your state bar may have guidance on how to word this.

## After someone presses send

Show a plain confirmation on the page: the message was received and what happens next. If the form also sends an email confirmation, keep it short and in the same tone.

Then test it. Fill in the form yourself on a phone, and confirm the message arrives where the firm will actually see it. A form that quietly fails is worse than no form.

## Keep it accessible

Every field needs a visible label, not just placeholder text that disappears when someone starts typing. Error messages should say what to fix in plain words. The W3C's [Web Content Accessibility Guidelines](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/) are the common public reference for this.
